1755 – 1900 · One galaxy, or many?

Island universes and the spiral nebulae

Immanuel Kant · William Herschel · Lord Rosse · James Keeler

What was known

The night sky is sprinkled with faint, fuzzy patches called nebulae. In 1755 the philosopher Immanuel Kant guessed that some were whole star systems like our own, so far away that their stars blur together. These were "island universes." William Herschel counted stars in different directions and mapped the Milky Way as a flattened "grindstone" with the Sun near its middle.

The idea

In 1845 Lord Rosse's giant 72-inch "Leviathan" telescope showed that some nebulae, like M51, had a spiral shape. A swirl looks like a rotating system of stars. But it could equally be a solar system forming out of gas, close by inside our own galaxy.

The test

Around 1898–1900 James Keeler photographed the sky with the Crossley reflector at Lick Observatory. Faint spiral nebulae turned up on almost every plate. He estimated that around 120,000 of them were within reach of his telescope.

Where things stood

No one could measure how far away a spiral nebula was. The key question was distance. If Andromeda was the same size as the Milky Way, its small angular size meant it had to be unimaginably far away. If it was small, it could be a neighbor. Until someone found a way to measure distances, either answer was possible.

If Andromeda were another Milky Way, how far would it be?

The Andromeda nebula spans about 3.2° of sky, about six full Moons. Pick how big you think a galaxy is. The small-angle formula then tells you how far Andromeda must be. Note that the answer depends entirely on your assumption. That's why the debate went in circles for decades.

Practice problems

1. Andromeda covers $\theta=3.2^\circ$. If it is the same size as the modern Milky Way, $D=100{,}000$ ly, how far away is it?

Show solution
  1. Convert the angle to radians. $\theta=3.2\times\dfrac{\pi}{180}=0.0559$ rad.
  2. Apply the small-angle formula. $d=\dfrac{D}{\theta}=\dfrac{100{,}000}{0.0559}=1.79\times10^{6}$ ly.
Answer: about $1.8$ million light-years. That's the same order of magnitude as the true $2.5$ Mly, and far beyond anything in the Milky Way.

2. Now assume Kapteyn's much smaller universe, $D=30{,}000$ ly. Where must Andromeda be, and does it still lie outside Kapteyn's universe?

Show solution
  1. Same formula. $d=\dfrac{30{,}000}{0.0559}=5.37\times10^{5}$ ly.
  2. Compare. $537{,}000$ ly is about $18\times$ the 30,000-ly diameter of the whole system.
Answer: about $540{,}000$ ly, still well outside. The island-universe idea stood or fell on whether the spirals really were the same size as the Milky Way.

3. In the spirit of Keeler's survey, suppose an average plate covering $1$ square degree shows $3$ faint spiral nebulae. The whole sky is $41{,}253$ square degrees. Estimate the total number in reach.

Show solution
  1. Scale by area. $N=3\ \text{per deg}^2\times41{,}253\ \text{deg}^2$.
  2. Evaluate. $N\approx1.24\times10^{5}$.
Answer: roughly $120{,}000$, the same order as Keeler's estimate. There were far too many of them to be a curiosity.
1908 – 1912 · A yardstick for the cosmos

Leavitt's law: a star's rhythm reveals its true brightness

Henrietta Swan Leavitt · Edward Pickering · the Harvard "computers"

What was known

A star's apparent magnitude mixes up two things: how luminous it really is and how far away it is. A faint star could be a dim one nearby or a brilliant one far away. Parallax could untangle the two, but only for stars within a few hundred light-years.

The idea

Leavitt was a Harvard "computer," paid a few cents an hour to compare photographic plates. She catalogued over 1,700 variable stars in the Magellanic Clouds. The trick was that every star in the Small Magellanic Cloud is at about the same distance from us. So differences in apparent brightness there are differences in true brightness.

The test

In 1912 she plotted 25 Cepheid variables in the Small Magellanic Cloud, brightness against pulsation period. The points fell on a clean line: the longer a Cepheid takes to pulse, the brighter it is.

What she found

This was the period–luminosity relation, now called Leavitt's law. Time a Cepheid's period and you know its true luminosity. Compare that to how bright it looks and you get its distance. It became the first standard candle that could reach across the cosmos. It only needed calibrating.

Why the Small Magellanic Cloud mattered. Leavitt had no idea how far the cloud was. She didn't need to know. Because all of its stars share one distance, the slope of her line is pure physics. Only the overall height of the line, the zero point, was unknown. Pinning that down became the next problem.

Practice problems

1. Using $M_V=-2.43(\log_{10}P-1)-4.05$, find the absolute magnitudes of Cepheids with $P=10$ d and $P=50$ d.

Show solution
  1. $P=10$ d. $\log_{10}10=1$, so $M_V=-2.43(0)-4.05=-4.05$.
  2. $P=50$ d. $\log_{10}50=1.699$, so $M_V=-2.43(0.699)-4.05=-1.70-4.05=-5.75$.
Answer: $-4.05$ and $-5.75$. The 50-day Cepheid is $1.7$ magnitudes (about $4.8\times$) more luminous.

2. In the Small Magellanic Cloud, a $30$-day Cepheid and a $3$-day Cepheid differ by one decade in period. By how many magnitudes do they differ, and what flux ratio is that?

Show solution
  1. Magnitude difference. $\Delta\log P=1$, so $\Delta m=2.43$.
  2. Flux ratio. $10^{0.4\times2.43}=10^{0.972}=9.4$.
Answer: $2.43$ mag, so the 30-day star is about $9.4\times$ brighter. Because both are in the same cloud, that's a ratio of true luminosity.

3. Two Cepheids have the same period, but B looks $5.0$ magnitudes fainter than A. How many times farther away is B?

Show solution
  1. Flux ratio. $5$ magnitudes $=10^{0.4\times5}=100\times$ fainter.
  2. Inverse-square law. $F\propto1/d^2$, so $d_B/d_A=\sqrt{100}=10$.
Answer: $10\times$ farther. Same period means same luminosity, so any extra faintness is distance. This is the logic Shapley and Hubble would use.
1913 · Pinning the yardstick down

Calibrating the Cepheids and a famous missing zero

Ejnar Hertzsprung · Henry Norris Russell · later Harlow Shapley

What was known

Leavitt's law gave relative brightness. To turn it into distances, someone needed the absolute magnitude of at least one Cepheid. But no Cepheid was close enough for direct parallax with 1913 equipment.

The idea

Hertzsprung used statistical parallax. Nearby Cepheids drift across the sky (proper motion) because of the Sun's own motion through space. If you average many of them, the size of that drift tells you their typical distance, and therefore their luminosity.

The test

From about a dozen Milky Way Cepheids he set the zero point and applied it to the Small Magellanic Cloud. The published paper gave the cloud's distance as about 3,000 light-years. That was apparently a slip for a figure about ten times larger.

The lesson

Even the intended figure, roughly 30,000 light-years, fell well short of today's 200,000. The zero point is where every error in the distance ladder starts. A mistake here shifts every Cepheid distance, and later every galaxy distance, by the same factor. Shapley recalibrated it in 1917–18, and its errors carried straight into his map of the Milky Way.

Practice problems

1. A 10-day Cepheid in the Small Magellanic Cloud has $m=15.0$ and, by today's calibration, $M=-4.05$. Ignoring dust, how far is the cloud?

Show solution
  1. Distance modulus. $m-M=15.0-(-4.05)=19.05$.
  2. Invert it. $d=10^{(19.05+5)/5}=10^{4.81}=6.46\times10^{4}$ pc.
  3. Convert to light-years. $6.46\times10^{4}\times3.26=2.1\times10^{5}$ ly.
Answer: about $210{,}000$ ly. That's seven times Hertzsprung's intended value and 70 times what the paper printed.

2. Suppose the zero point is wrong and Cepheids are actually $1.5$ mag brighter than you assumed. By what factor are all your Cepheid distances too small?

Show solution
  1. The modulus grows by 1.5. $d\propto10^{(m-M)/5}$.
  2. Factor. $10^{1.5/5}=10^{0.3}=2.0$.
Answer: a factor of $2$. That's almost exactly the correction Walter Baade found in 1952, which doubled the size of the known universe overnight.

3. The nearest bright Cepheid, Polaris, has a parallax of about $p=0.0075''$. How far is it, and why couldn't 1913 astronomers measure such parallaxes reliably (their precision was about $0.01''$)?

Show solution
  1. Parallax distance. $d=1/p=1/0.0075=133$ pc $\approx434$ ly.
  2. Compare with precision. The signal, $0.0075''$, is smaller than the measurement error of $0.01''$.
Answer: about $430$ ly. Even the nearest Cepheid's parallax was lost in the noise, which is why Hertzsprung had to fall back on statistics.
1912 – 1917 · The nebulae are on the move

Slipher clocks the spirals

Vesto Melvin Slipher · Percival Lowell · Lowell Observatory, Flagstaff

What was known

Stars in the Milky Way move at a few tens of km/s. The Doppler effect turns that motion into a tiny shift of spectral lines. A blueshift means the source is approaching; a redshift means it's receding.

The idea

Lowell wanted spectra of spirals because he suspected they were forming solar systems. Slipher made his spectrograph fast enough to record the faint light of a nebula, using exposures that ran over several nights.

The test

In late 1912 Andromeda's lines turned out to be shifted toward the blue by about 300 km/s. That was the fastest speed ever measured for any celestial object. Then he found the Sombrero nebula (M104) receding at about 1,000 km/s.

What he found

At the 1914 American Astronomical Society meeting he presented 15 spiral velocities and got a standing ovation. By 1917, 21 of his 25 spirals were redshifted, at an average of hundreds of km/s. Objects moving that fast seemed unlikely to be held by the Milky Way's gravity. It was the first clue to the expansion, though nobody yet knew what it meant.

Practice problems

1. In Andromeda's spectrum the calcium K line ($\lambda_0=393.37$ nm) appears at $392.98$ nm. Find the radial velocity.

Show solution
  1. Shift. $\Delta\lambda=392.98-393.37=-0.39$ nm.
  2. Doppler. $v=c\,\dfrac{\Delta\lambda}{\lambda_0}=299{,}792\times\dfrac{-0.39}{393.37}=-297$ km/s.
Answer: about $-300$ km/s. Andromeda is approaching, which is exactly Slipher's 1912 result.

2. The Sombrero recedes at $+1100$ km/s. Where does its K line appear?

Show solution
  1. Shift. $\Delta\lambda=393.37\times\dfrac{1100}{299{,}792}=1.44$ nm.
  2. Observed. $\lambda=393.37+1.44=394.81$ nm.
Answer: $394.81$ nm, a shift of about 1.4 nm. That's small, but huge for a celestial object of that era.

3. The Milky Way's escape speed near the Sun is roughly $550$ km/s. Slipher's 1917 average recession speed was about $570$ km/s. What did this hint about the spirals?

Show solution
  1. Compare. $570>550$ km/s, so a typical spiral is moving faster than the galaxy's gravity could hold.
  2. Interpret. Gravitationally bound members of the Milky Way shouldn't be racing away like this.
Answer: the spirals probably weren't bound to our galaxy. That was circumstantial support for island universes, years before anyone could measure their distances.
1914 – 1918 · Mount WilsonFocus · Shapley I

Shapley turns globular clusters into lighthouses

Harlow Shapley · Mount Wilson Observatory · the 60-inch reflector

What was known

A globular cluster is a tight ball of hundreds of thousands of stars. About a hundred were catalogued. Solon Bailey at Harvard had found that they swarm with short-period variable stars, which Shapley called "cluster variables" and we now call RR Lyrae stars. Leavitt's law, freshly calibrated, gave a way to read distances from pulsating stars.

The idea

Shapley arrived at Mount Wilson in 1914. He reasoned that if cluster variables are all roughly equally luminous, each one is a standard candle. Time it, measure its apparent magnitude, and the distance modulus gives the distance to the whole cluster.

The test

Using the 60-inch reflector, he measured cluster variables in the nearer clusters. He calibrated them with his own statistical-parallax zero point, treating them as short-period members of the Cepheid family. For clusters too remote for their variables to be seen, he built a ladder. Bright giant stars and then the clusters' apparent diameters stood in for the distance.

What he found

By 1918 he had distances for 69 globular clusters, and they were enormous: tens of thousands of light-years. No other method in 1918 reached that far. He now had a 3-D sample of the galaxy's outer reaches, ready to plot.

Why RR Lyrae stars work so well. Cepheids need a period to tell you their luminosity. RR Lyrae stars are simpler: they're old, low-mass stars burning helium on the horizontal branch, and nearly all have about the same luminosity, around 40–50 Suns. Find one, and you've effectively found a 45-Sun light bulb. Shapley's mistake wasn't the method. It was the wattage: his calibration made them about twice as bright as they really are.

The next rung: clusters too faint for variables

For distant clusters, individual variables were too faint to time. Shapley noticed that the nearby clusters, whose distances he knew, all had roughly similar true diameters. So he used each faint cluster's angular size as the yardstick. Try it: set the assumed true size and the measured angle.

Practice problems

1. The RR Lyrae stars in M3 have mean $V=15.65$. With today's $M_V=+0.6$ and extinction $A_V=0.03$, how far is M3?

Show solution
  1. True distance modulus. $m-M-A_V=15.65-0.6-0.03=15.02$.
  2. Invert. $d=10^{(15.02+5)/5}=10^{4.004}=1.01\times10^{4}$ pc.
  3. Light-years. $10.1\ \text{kpc}\times3262=3.3\times10^{4}$ ly.
Answer: about $10.1$ kpc, or $33{,}000$ ly. M3 sits high above the galactic plane, so dust barely matters.

2. Redo M3 the 1918 way: $M=-0.2$, no dust correction. By what factor does the distance grow?

Show solution
  1. Modulus. $15.65-(-0.2)=15.85$, so $d=10^{4.17}=1.48\times10^{4}$ pc $\approx48{,}000$ ly.
  2. Ratio. $14.8/10.1=1.47$, which is the same as $10^{(0.8+0.03)/5}$.
Answer: about $1.5\times$ too far. A calibration error of $0.8$ mag stretches every cluster distance by the same factor, which is why Shapley's Milky Way came out so huge.

3. A distant cluster spans $6.0$ arcmin. If globular clusters are about $30$ pc across, how far away is it?

Show solution
  1. Angle in radians. $\theta=\dfrac{6.0}{60}\times\dfrac{\pi}{180}=1.745\times10^{-3}$ rad.
  2. Small-angle distance. $d=\dfrac{30}{1.745\times10^{-3}}=1.72\times10^{4}$ pc.
Answer: about $17$ kpc, or $56{,}000$ ly. It's cruder than RR Lyrae, but it extended Shapley's map to clusters he couldn't otherwise reach.
1917 – 1918 · A map in three dimensionsFocus · Shapley II

Mapping the halo: the clusters don't surround the Sun

Harlow Shapley · building on Solon Bailey's and John Herschel's sky counts

What was known

It was an old puzzle that globular clusters aren't spread evenly over the sky. Almost all of them lie in one half of it, and about a third crowd into Sagittarius, Scorpius and Ophiuchus. Nobody had a good explanation.

The idea

With a distance for each cluster, Shapley could do more than note the lopsided sky. He combined each cluster's galactic longitude, galactic latitude and distance to plot it in 3-D. If the clusters belong to the galaxy, the centre of their swarm should mark the centre of the galaxy.

The test

He plotted the clusters as seen from above the galactic plane and edge-on. They didn't form a shell around the Sun. They formed a roughly spherical swarm, symmetric above and below the Milky Way's plane, whose centre lay far away toward Sagittarius.

What he found

This is the globular-cluster halo. Its centre is the galactic center. In 1918 he put it about 50,000 light-years away. The clusters were a scaffold outlining a galaxy far larger than Kapteyn's, and the Sun was nowhere near its middle.

Practice problems

1. Convert M13 ($l=59.0^\circ$, $b=40.9^\circ$, $d=7.1$ kpc) into Cartesian coordinates, with $x$ pointing toward Sagittarius.

Show solution
  1. In-plane part. $d\cos b=7.1\times0.756=5.37$ kpc.
  2. Components. $x=5.37\cos59^\circ=2.76$, $y=5.37\sin59^\circ=4.60$, $z=7.1\sin40.9^\circ=4.65$ kpc.
Answer: $(x,y,z)\approx(2.8,\,4.6,\,4.6)$ kpc. M13 is well above the plane, which is part of why it's so easy to see from the north.

2. A toy sample: four clusters lie along the Sun–Sagittarius line at $x=+5$, $+10$, $+12$ and $-3$ kpc. Where is the centre of the swarm, and in which direction?

Show solution
  1. Average. $\bar x=\dfrac{5+10+12-3}{4}=\dfrac{24}{4}=6.0$ kpc.
  2. Interpret. It's positive, so it lies toward Sagittarius.
Answer: $6$ kpc toward Sagittarius. With clusters on both sides of the centre, their average lands on the centre, not on us. That is the whole argument in one line.

3. Shapley placed the centre at about $15$ kpc; today's value is $8.2$ kpc. His RR Lyrae calibration was $0.8$ mag too bright. How much of the discrepancy does that explain, and how much dust (in magnitudes) would account for the rest?

Show solution
  1. Calibration factor. $10^{0.8/5}=1.45$, so $8.2\times1.45=11.9$ kpc.
  2. Remaining factor. $15/11.9=1.26$, and $5\log_{10}1.26=0.50$ mag.
Answer: the calibration explains a stretch from $8.2$ to $12$ kpc. About half a magnitude of uncorrected dust dimming, which is typical for clusters near the plane, explains the rest.
1918 – 1930 · Copernicus, part twoFocus · Shapley III

Dethroning the Sun

Harlow Shapley · vs. Jacobus Kapteyn · later Robert Trumpler (1930)

What was known

Jacobus Kapteyn had spent decades counting stars in every direction. His "Kapteyn universe" was a flattened system about 30,000–40,000 light-years across, with the Sun close to its centre. Star counts thinned out evenly whichever way he looked.

The idea

Shapley's clusters told a different story. If the halo is centred 50,000 light-years away toward Sagittarius, then the Sun is far out in the galaxy's suburbs. In that case Kapteyn's star counts were being cut off by something.

The test

The answer was dust. In 1930 Robert Trumpler showed that distant open clusters look dimmer than their size implies. Interstellar dust absorbs starlight along the galactic plane like a fog, so star counts in the plane only reach a few thousand light-years in any direction. That's why Kapteyn appeared to be at the centre. The globular clusters, which mostly lie high above or below the plane, could be seen over the fog.

What it meant

The Sun isn't at the centre of the universe, or even of the Milky Way. Shapley moved us out of the centre as decisively as Copernicus had moved us off it. His scale was too large: the true distance to the centre is about 26,000 light-years. But his overall picture was right. The Sun lies in a minor spur, the Orion Arm, between larger spiral arms. Those arms were mapped only in the 1950s, long after Shapley.

Practice problems

1. The Sun orbits the galactic center at $v=230$ km/s at $R_0=8.2$ kpc ($1$ kpc $=3.086\times10^{16}$ km). How long is one orbit?

Show solution
  1. Circumference. $2\pi R_0=2\pi\times8.2\times3.086\times10^{16}=1.59\times10^{18}$ km.
  2. Period. $P=\dfrac{1.59\times10^{18}}{230}=6.91\times10^{15}$ s $=\dfrac{6.91\times10^{15}}{3.156\times10^{7}}=2.2\times10^{8}$ yr.
Answer: about $220$ million years. The Sun has made only about $20$ laps since it formed.

2. Treating the orbit as circular, $M_{\text{enc}}=v^2R_0/G$. How much mass lies inside the Sun's orbit?

Show solution
  1. SI units. $v=2.3\times10^{5}$ m/s, $R_0=8.2\times3.086\times10^{19}=2.53\times10^{20}$ m.
  2. Evaluate. $M=\dfrac{(2.3\times10^{5})^2(2.53\times10^{20})}{6.674\times10^{-11}}=2.0\times10^{41}$ kg $=1.0\times10^{11}\,M_\odot$.
Answer: about $10^{11}$ solar masses. Shapley had placed us in the outskirts of a truly vast system.

3. A star with $M=+1$ lies $2$ kpc away in the plane, where dust adds $a=1.5$ mag/kpc. Find its apparent magnitude with and without dust, and the dimming factor.

Show solution
  1. No dust. $m=1+5\log_{10}(2000/10)=1+11.5=12.5$.
  2. With dust. Add $a\,d=1.5\times2=3.0$, so $m=15.5$.
  3. Dimming. $10^{0.4\times3}=15.8\times$.
Answer: $12.5\rightarrow15.5$, about $16\times$ fainter. Star counts run out within a few kiloparsecs in every direction, which created Kapteyn's illusion that he was at the centre.
1916 – 1921 · A measurement that misled

Van Maanen's spinning spirals

Adriaan van Maanen · Mount Wilson · Shapley's ally

What was known

Van Maanen was a respected expert at measuring proper motion: the tiny sideways drift of stars across the sky, found by comparing photographic plates taken years apart.

The idea

If spiral nebulae rotate, comparing old and new plates should show knots in their arms shifting along the spiral. The angular rate, combined with the distance, gives a physical speed.

The test

From 1916 on he reported motions of about 0.02″ per year in M101 and other spirals. If real, a spiral millions of light-years away would have to spin faster than light. So spirals must be small and nearby.

The verdict

The motions weren't real. They were systematic errors at the edge of what plates and measuring engines could detect. Still, Shapley trusted his friend's numbers, and they reinforced his view that spirals were local. The episode held up acceptance of island universes for years. In 1935 Hubble's own remeasurements showed no rotation could be detected.

April 26, 1920 · National Academy of Sciences

The Great Debate: Shapley vs. Curtis

Harlow Shapley (Mount Wilson) · Heber Curtis (Lick Observatory)

The setting

The National Academy in Washington staged a debate on "The Scale of the Universe." Shapley defended his Big Galaxy: a Milky Way 300,000 light-years across that contains everything, spirals included. Curtis defended a smaller Milky Way and argued that the spirals are island universes far beyond it.

Shapley's case

Globular-cluster distances made the galaxy enormous. If the spirals were comparable galaxies, they would have to be absurdly far away. Van Maanen's rotations put them nearby. And the 1885 "nova" S Andromedae would have been impossibly luminous at an island-universe distance.

Curtis's case

Novae seen in spirals were far fainter than novae in the Milky Way, so the spirals must be distant. Spiral spectra look like the combined light of many stars. Slipher's enormous velocities didn't fit Milky Way objects. And the dark dust lanes seen across edge-on spirals matched the Milky Way's own.

The outcome

Neither side won outright. Each was right about something and wrong about something else. Shapley left for the Harvard College Observatory directorship in 1921, leaving the 100-inch telescope at Mount Wilson to Edwin Hubble.

October 1923 – January 1, 1925 · The day we found the universe

"VAR!": a Cepheid in Andromeda

Edwin Hubble · Milton Humason · the 100-inch Hooker telescope · Henry Norris Russell

What was known

The 100-inch Hooker telescope, the largest in the world, could resolve the outskirts of nearby spirals into individual stars. Hubble began photographing Andromeda, looking for novae.

The idea

On plate H335H, taken on the night of October 5–6, 1923, he marked three new spots with "N" for nova. Comparing with older plates, he saw that one of them brightened and faded repeatedly. It wasn't a nova. It was a Cepheid.

The test

He crossed out the "N" and wrote "VAR!" The star had a period of about 31 days. Leavitt's law, with Shapley's own calibration, gave a distance of nearly a million light-years. That's far beyond even Shapley's huge Milky Way.

What he found

Andromeda is a galaxy in its own right. Hubble wrote to Shapley in early 1924. On reading the letter, Shapley reportedly said: "Here is the letter that has destroyed my universe." Wary of van Maanen's results, Hubble held off publishing. His paper was finally read for him at the joint AAS–AAAS meeting in Washington on January 1, 1925. That's the day of Bartusiak's title.

1917 – 1931 · The universe is not standing still

From island universes to an expanding universe

Albert Einstein · Willem de Sitter · Alexander Friedmann · Georges Lemaître · Edwin Hubble & Milton Humason

What was known

In 1917 Einstein applied general relativity to the whole cosmos. To keep the universe static, as everyone assumed it was, he added a cosmological constant. De Sitter's alternative solution that same year predicted that distant light would be redshifted. Friedmann (1922) showed that relativity naturally allows a universe that expands or contracts.

The idea

In 1927 Lemaître, a Belgian priest and physicist, combined an expanding solution with Slipher's redshifts and Hubble's distances. He predicted that velocity should grow in proportion to distance and estimated the rate. He published in an obscure Belgian journal, and almost no one noticed.

The test

In 1929 Hubble plotted velocity against distance for 24 galaxies, using Cepheid and brightest-star distances and mostly Slipher's velocities. The trend was rough but clear. By 1931 Humason's far fainter spectra had pushed it out to about 20,000 km/s.

What they found

The redshift–distance law, now Hubble's law (formally renamed the Hubble–Lemaître law in 2018), is v = H₀ d. Space itself is expanding. When Eddington arranged an English translation of Lemaître's paper in 1931, the section with his rate estimate was left out. In 1931 Einstein visited Mount Wilson and accepted the expansion.

Answer: $1400$ km/s and $395.2$ nm. The same kind of measurement Slipher made in 1912 now reads out cosmic distance.
