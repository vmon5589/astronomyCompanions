~350 – 240 BC · AlexandriaRung 1 · 6,400 km

A stick, a shadow and the size of the Earth

Aristotle · Eratosthenes of Cyrene

What was known

Aristotle had already argued that the Earth is a sphere. During a lunar eclipse, Earth's shadow on the Moon is always a circular arc, and travellers going south see new stars rise above the horizon. The Sun is so far away that its rays arrive essentially parallel.

The idea

Eratosthenes heard that at noon on the summer solstice, the Sun shone straight down a well at Syene (modern Aswan) and cast no shadow. If at that same moment a stick in Alexandria, to the north, did cast a shadow, the angle of that shadow must equal the angle between the two cities as seen from Earth's centre.

The test

In Alexandria the shadow angle was about 7.2°, one-fiftieth of a full circle. The cities were about 5,000 stadia apart, a distance measured by pacing or by camel-caravan travel times.

What he found

The circumference is 50 × 5,000 = 250,000 stadia. Depending on which stadion he used, that's about 39,000–46,000 km, against today's 40,075 km. The first rung of the ladder needs only parallel rays, one angle and one measured distance.

Practice problems

1. The shadow angle is $7.2^\circ$ and the cities are $800$ km apart. Find Earth's circumference and radius.

Show solution
  1. Fraction of a circle. $7.2/360=1/50$.
  2. Circumference. $C=50\times800=40{,}000$ km.
  3. Radius. $R=40{,}000/2\pi=6{,}366$ km.
Answer: $40{,}000$ km and $6{,}370$ km. That's within a fraction of a percent of the truth, though partly by luck.

2. If Eratosthenes meant the Attic stadion ($185$ m), what circumference did he find for $250{,}000$ stadia, and how far off is that?

Show solution
  1. Convert. $250{,}000\times0.185=46{,}250$ km.
  2. Error. $46{,}250/40{,}075=1.154$.
Answer: $46{,}250$ km, about $15\%$ too large. Even so, it's an astonishing result for 240 BC, and the method is flawless.

3. You repeat the experiment between two towns $555$ km apart on the same meridian and find a shadow-angle difference of $5.0^\circ$. What circumference do you get?

Show solution
  1. Ratio. $360/5.0=72$.
  2. Circumference. $C=72\times555=39{,}960$ km.
Answer: about $40{,}000$ km. The same geometry works anywhere, provided both places lie roughly north–south of each other.
~270 – 150 BC · Aristarchus & HipparchusRung 2 · 384,000 km

A lunar eclipse measures the Moon

Aristarchus of Samos · Hipparchus

What was known

From the first rung we have Earth's radius. During a lunar eclipse the Moon passes through Earth's shadow. The Moon also looks about half a degree across, and the Sun looks almost exactly the same size, which is why total solar eclipses are just barely total.

The idea

Time the eclipse. The Moon moves through the sky at roughly its own diameter per hour. Compare how long it takes to cross the shadow with how long it takes to move one Moon-width, and you've measured the shadow's width in Moon diameters.

The test

The umbra at the Moon's distance is about 2.6 Moon diameters wide. Because the Sun isn't a point, the shadow cone tapers. Since the Sun and Moon look the same size, the cone shrinks by about one Moon diameter over the Earth–Moon distance. So Earth is about 2.6 + 1 ≈ 3.7 Moon diameters across.

What they found

The Moon's radius is about 1,700 km. Its 0.5° angular diameter then gives the distance: about 380,000 km, or 60 Earth radii. Hipparchus got close to this value around 150 BC. That's rung 2.

Practice problems

1. The umbra is $2.6$ Moon diameters wide. With the taper correction, and $R_\oplus=6371$ km, what is the Moon's radius?

Show solution
  1. Earth in Moon diameters. $2.6+1=3.6$.
  2. Moon radius. $R_{☾}=6371/3.6=1770$ km.
Answer: about $1{,}770$ km. The true value is $1{,}737$ km, a 2% error from a stopwatch and a shadow.

2. The Moon spans $0.52^\circ$. Using $R_{☾}=1770$ km, how far away is it?

Show solution
  1. Angle in radians. $0.52\times\pi/180=9.08\times10^{-3}$.
  2. Distance. $d=\dfrac{2\times1770}{9.08\times10^{-3}}=3.90\times10^{5}$ km.
Answer: about $390{,}000$ km, or $61$ Earth radii (true: $384{,}400$ km).

3. The Moon circles the sky once every $27.3$ days. How many degrees does it move per hour, and how long does it take to move its own diameter ($0.52^\circ$)?

Show solution
  1. Rate. $\dfrac{360^\circ}{27.3\times24\text{ h}}=0.549^\circ$/h.
  2. Own diameter. $0.52/0.549=0.95$ h.
Answer: about one Moon-width per hour. That's the stopwatch that turns eclipse timings into a size.
~270 BC · AristarchusRung 3 · 150 million km

The half Moon and the distance to the Sun

Aristarchus of Samos

What was known

The Moon shines by reflected sunlight. When it is exactly half lit, at first or last quarter, the Sun–Moon–Earth angle must be a perfect right angle.

The idea

At that moment, measure the angle between the Moon and the Sun as seen from Earth. Together with the right angle at the Moon, it fixes the shape of the triangle. And we already know one side from rung 2: the Earth–Moon distance.

The test

Aristarchus measured 87°, so the Sun is 1/cos 87° ≈ 19 times farther than the Moon. The real angle is 89.85°. It's so close to 90° that the method is extremely sensitive: a tiny error in the angle makes a huge difference in the result.

What he found

His number was 20 times too small, but his conclusion was right. Since the Sun and Moon look the same size, the Sun must be as many times larger as it is farther. Even at 19×, the Sun dwarfed the Earth. Aristarchus concluded that the big Sun, not the small Earth, should sit at the centre. That was 1,800 years before Copernicus.

Practice problems

1. With Aristarchus' angle of $87^\circ$, how many times farther is the Sun than the Moon?

Show solution
  1. Ratio. $1/\cos87^\circ=1/0.0523=19.1$.
Answer: about $19$, which matches his famous result of "between 18 and 20."

2. With the true angle $89.85^\circ$ and $d_{☾}=384{,}400$ km, find the Sun's distance.

Show solution
  1. Cosine. $\cos89.85^\circ=\sin0.15^\circ=2.62\times10^{-3}$, so the ratio is $382$.
  2. Distance. $382\times384{,}400=1.47\times10^{8}$ km.
Answer: about $147$ million km, close to the true 1 AU of $149.6$ million km.

3. Show how sensitive this is. What ratio do you get at $89.75^\circ$, only $0.1^\circ$ less?

Show solution
  1. Ratio. $1/\cos89.75^\circ=1/\sin0.25^\circ=1/4.36\times10^{-3}=229$.
Answer: $229$ instead of $382$, a 40% change from a tenth of a degree. Tao's point: the geometry was perfect, but the measurement was beyond the instruments of the day. It took telescopes and a different method (rung 6) to get it right.
1543 · De revolutionibusRung 4 · solar system in AU

Copernicus scales the planets in AU

Nicolaus Copernicus

What was known

Mercury and Venus never stray far from the Sun. Venus is always an evening or morning "star," never seen at midnight. Mars, Jupiter and Saturn sometimes appear opposite the Sun and loop backwards across the sky.

The idea

Put the Sun at the centre and these oddities become geometry. An inner planet's greatest angle from the Sun, its maximum elongation, happens when our line of sight just grazes its orbit. That makes a right triangle with the Sun.

The test

Venus reaches about 46° from the Sun. So its orbital radius is sin 46° ≈ 0.72 of Earth's. For outer planets, the time between oppositions (the synodic period) gives the true orbital period. Then their geometry at chosen dates gives their distances.

What he found

Copernicus found the relative layout of the whole solar system, in units of the Earth–Sun distance, the astronomical unit. His results were close to modern values. The shape was solved, but its size in kilometres was still unknown.

Practice problems

1. Venus' greatest elongation is $46.3^\circ$. What is its orbital radius?

Show solution
  1. Right triangle. $r=\sin46.3^\circ=0.723$ AU.
Answer: $0.72$ AU, exactly the modern value.

2. Mars returns to opposition every $780$ days. Find its true orbital period.

Show solution
  1. Synodic relation. $\dfrac{1}{P}=\dfrac{1}{365.25}-\dfrac{1}{780}=0.0027379-0.0012821=0.0014558$.
  2. Invert. $P=687$ days.
Answer: $687$ days. That number is the key to the next rung.

3. Jupiter's synodic period is $399$ days. Find its period, then its distance using Kepler's third law $a^3=P^2$.

Show solution
  1. Period. $\dfrac{1}{P}=\dfrac{1}{365.25}-\dfrac{1}{398.9}=2.31\times10^{-4}$, so $P=4330$ d $=11.86$ yr.
  2. Distance. $a=11.86^{2/3}=5.20$ AU.
Answer: $11.9$ years and $5.2$ AU.
1600 – 1619 · PragueRung 5 · true orbit shapes

Kepler's 687-day trick: triangulating Mars

Tycho Brahe's data · Johannes Kepler

What was known

Tycho Brahe left decades of naked-eye positions of Mars, accurate to about 1–2 arcminutes. Every observation is just a direction from a moving Earth. There's no depth. How do you turn directions into a map?

The idea

Mars takes 687 days to orbit the Sun. So observations exactly 687 days apart show Mars at the same point in space, while Earth is in two different places. Two sightlines from two known Earth positions cross at Mars, the same way surveyors triangulate. Kepler also turned this around: by holding Mars fixed, he first mapped Earth's own orbit.

The test

Pair after pair, he plotted points on Mars' orbit. They didn't lie on a circle. They missed by 8 arcminutes, an error Kepler refused to ignore because he trusted Tycho's data.

What he found

Mars moves on an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. These are Kepler's laws: the ellipse, equal areas in equal times, and later P² = a³. The solar system now had its true shapes, scaled in AU.

Practice problems

1. In $687$ days, how far around its orbit does Earth get? How far apart are the two Earth positions (the baseline)?

Show solution
  1. Orbits. $687/365.25=1.881$, so Earth ends $0.881\times360^\circ=317^\circ$ around, or $43^\circ$ short of a second full lap.
  2. Chord. $2\sin(43^\circ/2)=2\sin21.5^\circ=0.733$ AU.
Answer: a baseline of about $0.73$ AU. That's a solid triangle for surveying Mars.

2. Kepler's points give Mars' closest and farthest distances from the Sun as $1.381$ and $1.666$ AU. Find $a$ and $e$.

Show solution
  1. Semi-major axis. $a=(1.381+1.666)/2=1.5235$ AU.
  2. Eccentricity. $e=(1.666-1.381)/(1.666+1.381)=0.0935$.
Answer: $a=1.52$ AU and $e=0.093$. That's small, but at Tycho's precision it was unmistakably not a circle.

3. Check Kepler's third law for Mars: $P=1.881$ yr, $a=1.5237$ AU.

Show solution
  1. Compare. $P^2=3.538$ and $a^3=3.537$.
Answer: they agree to 0.03%. One law connects every planet's period to its distance.
1672 – 1769 · Paris, Cayenne and the transits of VenusRung 6 · 1 AU = 149.6 million km

Putting kilometres on the map

Giovanni Cassini & Jean Richer · Ole Rømer · the transit-of-Venus expeditions

What was known

After Kepler, every planetary distance was known as a ratio of the AU. Measure any one of them in kilometres, and the whole solar system is scaled at once.

The idea

Use the Earth itself as a baseline. Two observers far apart on Earth see a nearby planet in slightly different directions against the stars. The baseline comes from rung 1, Earth's size. Kepler's geometry gives the planet's distance in AU. The angle gives that distance in km.

The test

In 1672 Cassini in Paris and Richer in Cayenne (French Guiana) observed Mars at opposition, about 0.38 AU away. The shift was only about 25 arcseconds. Later, the transits of Venus in 1761 and 1769 sent expeditions across the globe to sharpen the measurement.

What they found

An AU of about 140 million km, within 10% of the true 149.6 million. Soon afterwards, in 1676, Rømer noticed that Jupiter's moon Io kept "running late" when Jupiter was far from Earth. The delay was the time light takes to cross the extra distance, which gave the first measurement of the speed of light. Today radar pings to Venus pin the AU to the metre.

Practice problems

1. The modern solar parallax is $\pi_\odot=8.794''$ (the angle Earth's radius, $6378$ km, subtends from the Sun). Find 1 AU.

Show solution
  1. Radians. $8.794/206{,}265=4.263\times10^{-5}$.
  2. Distance. $6378/4.263\times10^{-5}=1.496\times10^{8}$ km.
Answer: $149.6$ million km.

2. Cassini's result corresponded to a solar parallax of about $9.5''$. What AU does that give, and what's the error?

Show solution
  1. AU. $6378/(9.5/206{,}265)=1.385\times10^{8}$ km.
  2. Error. $138.5/149.6=0.926$.
Answer: about $138$ million km, 7% low. Like Eratosthenes, a huge leap forward.

3. Light takes $1000$ s to cross Earth's orbit (2 AU). Find $c$. What did Rømer's 22-minute estimate imply?

Show solution
  1. Modern. $c=2\times1.496\times10^{8}/1000=2.99\times10^{5}$ km/s.
  2. Rømer. $2\times1.496\times10^{8}/1320=2.27\times10^{5}$ km/s.
Answer: $3.0\times10^{5}$ km/s, versus about $2.3\times10^{5}$ from Rømer's numbers. That was the first proof that light has a finite speed, and it's a value we'll need at the redshift rung.
1838 → Gaia · Königsberg to orbitRung 7 · tens of thousands of ly

Stellar parallax: the AU as a baseline

Friedrich Bessel · Thomas Henderson · Friedrich Struve · Hipparcos · Gaia

What was known

If Earth orbits the Sun, nearby stars should shift back and forth against distant ones each year. The ancient Greeks saw no such shift. That was one of their strongest arguments against a moving Earth. It worked only if the stars were unimaginably far away, and they turned out to be.

The idea

Use Earth's orbit as the baseline: 2 AU, now known in km from rung 6. Pick a star that's likely to be close, one with a large proper motion, and measure its position six months apart.

The test

In 1838 Bessel measured 61 Cygni's parallax as 0.314″. That's the width of a coin seen from about 15 km away. Henderson (α Centauri) and Struve (Vega) reported results at about the same time.

What he found

61 Cygni is about 10 light-years away (modern value 11.4). The parsec, the distance at which parallax equals one arcsecond, became the unit of the stars. Today the Gaia satellite measures parallaxes to about 0.02 milliarcseconds for over a billion stars, and those parallaxes calibrate every rung above.

1. 61 Cygni's modern parallax is $0.286''$. How far is it in parsecs and light-years?

Show solution
  1. Invert. $d=1/0.286=3.50$ pc $=3.50\times3.26=11.4$ ly.

2. Tycho Brahe could measure about $1'=60''$. How close would a star need to be for Tycho to see its parallax? What did he conclude when he saw none?

Show solution
  1. Distance. $d=1/60$ pc $=206{,}265/60\approx3{,}400$ AU.
  2. His choice. Either the stars were more than thousands of AU away, which seemed absurdly empty, or Earth didn't move.
Answer: within about $3{,}400$ AU. Tycho chose a stationary Earth. The real answer was the "absurd" one, since the nearest star is $270{,}000$ AU away.

3. Gaia's precision for a bright star is about $0.02$ mas. To measure a distance to $10\%$, the parallax must be $10\times$ that error. How far can Gaia reach at $10\%$?

Show solution
  1. Smallest usable parallax. $p=0.2$ mas $=2\times10^{-4}{}''$.
  2. Distance. $d=1/2\times10^{-4}=5000$ pc.
Answer: about $5$ kpc, or $16{,}000$ ly. That's a good part of the Milky Way, and plenty of Cepheids to calibrate the next rung.
1908 – today · Stars of known wattageRung 8a · up to ~150 million ly

Stellar standard candles

Ejnar Hertzsprung · Henrietta Leavitt · Harlow Shapley · Walter Baade · Wendy Freedman · Barry Madore

What was known

Light spreads over a growing sphere, so brightness falls as 1/d². If you knew a star's true luminosity, its apparent brightness would give its distance. But parallax gives luminosities only for stars within a few thousand light-years.

The idea

Find kinds of stars whose luminosity can be recognised from far away, by their colour, their pulsation period or their place in a population. Calibrate the nearby examples with parallax, and every distant example becomes a distance marker.

The test

Hertzsprung's colour–magnitude diagrams allowed main-sequence fitting of whole clusters. Leavitt's 1912 period–luminosity law turned Cepheids into the workhorse candle. In 1952 Baade found that "Cepheids" were really two populations, and correcting the mix-up doubled the distance scale. Modern alternatives include the tip of the red giant branch (TRGB) and carbon-rich J-region AGB stars (JAGB).

What they give

Several independent stellar candles that overlap. Cepheids, TRGB and JAGB all reach galaxies tens of megaparsecs away, far enough to calibrate the supernovae of the next rung. Comparing them is one of the main ways to test the Hubble tension.

Practice problems

1. A cluster's main sequence lines up with the calibrated one after a shift of $m-M=5.65$. How far is the cluster? Compare with Gaia's Pleiades parallax of $7.36$ mas.

Show solution
  1. Fitting. $d=10^{(5.65+5)/5}=10^{2.13}=135$ pc.
  2. Parallax. $d=1/0.00736=136$ pc.
Answer: about $135$ pc either way. The two independent methods agree, and that agreement is what lets the ladder go higher.

2. A $10$-day classical Cepheid ($M=-4.05$) appears at $m=25.0$. How far away is its galaxy? If it were mistaken for a Type II Cepheid (about $1.5$ mag fainter), what distance would you get?

Show solution
  1. Correct. $\mu=29.05$, so $d=10^{6.81}=6.5$ Mpc.
  2. Mistaken. $M=-2.55$ gives $\mu=27.55$, so $d=10^{6.51}=3.2$ Mpc.
Answer: $6.5$ Mpc, versus $3.2$ Mpc if the populations are confused. That factor of 2 is Baade's 1952 correction.

3. In a galaxy's halo, the red giant branch stops sharply at $I=26.0$. Using $M_I^{\text{TRGB}}=-4.05$, find the distance.

Show solution
  1. Modulus. $26.0+4.05=30.05$.
  2. Distance. $d=10^{(30.05+5)/5}=10^{7.01}=1.02\times10^{7}$ pc.
Answer: about $10$ Mpc. TRGB works in old populations with little dust, a useful check on Cepheids, which live in dusty young disks.
1930s – today · Candles for whole galaxiesRung 8b · billions of ly

Brighter candles: whole galaxies and exploding stars

Brent Tully & Richard Fisher · Mark Phillips · the High-z and Supernova Cosmology teams · LIGO/Virgo

What was known

Even the brightest single stars fade from view beyond about 50–100 megaparsecs. To go further you need something far more luminous: a whole galaxy, or a star that explodes.

The idea

Look for relations that predict luminosity. A spiral galaxy that rotates faster is more massive and brighter (Tully–Fisher). Elliptical galaxies obey a "fundamental plane" linking size, brightness and velocity dispersion. And a supernova's light curve reveals its peak brightness.

The test

Each method is calibrated with galaxies whose distances are already known from Cepheids, TRGB or JAGB. In 1993 Mark Phillips showed that Type Ia supernovae that fade faster are intrinsically dimmer. Correcting for this makes them precise to about 5–7% each.

What they give

Distances to billions of light-years. In 1998 standardized Type Ia supernovae revealed that the expansion is accelerating, which earned the 2011 Nobel Prize. In 2017 the neutron-star merger GW170817 provided the first "standard siren," a distance from gravitational waves alone, with no ladder.

2. A Type Ia supernova peaks at $B=16.0$ and has $\Delta m_{15}=1.5$. Find its distance with the Phillips correction, then without it ($M=-19.3$).

Show solution

Redshift: the top rung

Vesto Slipher · Edwin Hubble · Georges Lemaître

What was known

From 1912 Slipher found that most galaxies' spectral lines are shifted toward the red. Hubble then measured their distances with Cepheids, one rung down.

The idea

Plot redshift velocity against candle distance. In 1929 the relation turned out to be linear: v = H₀ d. The universe is expanding. Once you've calibrated H₀ with candles, a galaxy's redshift alone gives its distance, even when there's no candle to see.

The test

A spectrum is easy to measure, even for a very faint galaxy. Redshift surveys now map millions of galaxies. At large distances "distance" has several meanings and the full cosmological model is needed, but for nearby galaxies d ≈ cz / H₀.

Where it stands

The ladder gives H₀ ≈ 73 km/s/Mpc. The cosmic microwave background, interpreted with the standard model of cosmology, gives 67.4. The gap, about 8%, is called the Hubble tension. Either some rung hides a systematic error, or there's new physics.

Putting it together

The whole ladder, and how errors climb it

Every rung, from a shadow in Alexandria to the edge of the observable universe

The structure

Each rung borrows its yardstick from the one below. Earth's radius gives the Moon. The Moon gives the Sun, at least in principle. Kepler gives ratios, parallax across the Earth gives kilometres, the AU gives stellar parallax, parallax calibrates Cepheids, Cepheids calibrate supernovae, and supernovae calibrate H₀.

The lesson

Tao's point is that ingenuity beats instruments. Most rungs were climbed with simple geometry long before precise tools existed. But a ladder is only as strong as its weakest rung, so errors that start low are carried all the way up.

Cross-checks

Modern astronomy builds redundancy. Radar ranging checks the AU. Gaia checks parallaxes. Tip-of-the-red-giant-branch stars and masers cross-check Cepheids. And the CMB gives an independent route to H₀ that skips the ladder entirely.

What's at stake

If two independent routes disagree, as they do in the Hubble tension, either one route has a hidden systematic error or our model of the universe is incomplete.

1. Gaia parallaxes contribute $1.0\%$, the Cepheid relation $1.5\%$ and the supernova calibration $2.0\%$. What is the combined uncertainty if they're independent? What if they're fully systematic?

Show solution

2. Eratosthenes' 15% error in Earth's radius: if a modern ladder somehow still depended on it at full strength, how would it affect a galaxy distance of $100$ Mpc?

Show solution

  2. Apply. $100\times1.15=115$ Mpc.
Answer: $115$ Mpc. That's why each base rung has been re-measured independently. The AU is now known from radar to about a metre.
